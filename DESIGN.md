# A public-space workshop that can explain its decisions

## Problem and intended users

Community groups, municipal designers and schoolyard facilitators need a shared way to compare a limited budget with competing needs. A drawing communicates a vision; a spreadsheet lists costs. Neither, by itself, shows which combination of commitments makes a design impossible. Common Ground joins an approachable visual concept with a transparent bounded decision model.

The illustrative scenario concerns a 600 m² pocket park. The user chooses minimum commitments to shade, access and rest, play and belonging, and water stewardship. The solver does not decide whose needs matter. It makes the consequences of the chosen inputs visible.

## Research grounding

- [WHO, Urban green space interventions and health: review of impacts and effectiveness](https://www.who.int/europe/publications/m/item/urban-green-space-interventions-and-health--a-review-of-impacts-and-effectiveness.-full-report). Physical improvements and social engagement should be considered together. We use this to motivate the workshop format, not to calculate health outcomes.
- [WHO, Urban green spaces: a brief for action](https://www.who.int/europe/publications/i/item/9789289052498). A practical planning reference for local decision-makers. It supports involving stakeholders and considering access and equity; our score numbers are not extracted from this brief.
- [WHO, GreenUr](https://www.who.int/europe/tools-and-toolkits/greenur--the-green-urban-spaces-and-health-tool). Existing city-scale green-space assessment is an adjacent approach. Common Ground's narrow contribution is the small-project negotiation interface and verified conflict explanations, not a claim to replace geographic or health assessment.

Sources checked September 21, 2026. The current prototype is desk research plus an implemented design experiment, not validated demand or measured impact.

## Service design

**Before the workshop:** a facilitator and local specialist replace the catalog's illustrative costs and units with survey-backed options. Residents can review materials in advance, in accessible language and formats.

**During:** participants set minimum requirements, protect choices and inspect alternatives. A conflict is used to open a conversation, not tell a minority group to abandon its needs. Lowering accessibility requirements is never an automatic action; an actual legal/design access requirement must be encoded as non-negotiable in a real version.

**After:** export assumptions, chosen interventions and unresolved questions. A designer checks constructability, approvals, access, drainage, lifecycle cost and maintenance responsibilities. The studio has no authority to authorize spending or construction.

## Illustrative pilot and operating model

A proposed, uncommitted pilot could involve one neighborhood organization, one designer and two facilitated sessions of 8–12 residents. No partnership, customer commitment or participation has been obtained. An illustrative facilitation budget is €1,500–€3,000, separate from construction. This estimate is a hypothesis to test with local providers, not a quoted price.

Success measures to collect prospectively: can participants explain a tradeoff without help; do all groups understand whose priorities changed; time to reach a documented shortlist; number of unresolved assumptions surfaced; and agreement that the record fairly represents the discussion. Do not substitute time-to-consensus for equitable participation.

A possible future model is open-source core software with paid catalog preparation, facilitation and municipal integration. The project currently has no revenue, users, partners, or validated willingness to pay.

## Why the design is distinctive

The primary interaction is not a chatbot or opaque recommendation. A person changes a real constraint and sees the entire possibility space respond. An impossible request has an inspectable explanation, a reproducible certificate, and exact single-change repair options. The illustration gives the tradeoff a place; the arithmetic makes it checkable.

## Risks and limitations

Illustrative additive scores can conceal interactions and differing needs. Equal weights are a demonstrator assumption. Tree shade changes over years; canopy shade is immediate. Spatial units do not enforce actual geometry. Costs exclude unmodeled site works and approvals. Care units are ordinal placeholders. The current public prototype cannot edit the catalog through the UI. A real pilot needs local inputs, accessible facilitation, non-negotiable design constraints, uncertainty ranges and testing with residents. No environmental or health benefit is quantified.
